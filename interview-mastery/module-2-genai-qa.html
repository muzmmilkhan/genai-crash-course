Phase 1: questions (15)

Q1What is Generative AI?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • A crisp one-line definition, not a buzzword tour
  • You know it is probabilistic: it samples from a learned distribution
  • A concrete example right after the definition
🗣️ Say this

Generative AI is AI that creates new content, such as text, images, audio, code or structured data, instead of only labelling existing data. It learns the statistical patterns of a huge training set and then samples new outputs that resemble the data without copying it. A language model, for example, estimates the probability of the next token given everything before it, and by sampling repeatedly it writes paragraphs or working code. Image models like Stable Diffusion instead learn to turn random noise into a picture guided by a text description. ChatGPT drafting an email is the everyday example.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Text models model P(next token | previous tokens); generation is repeated sampling from that distribution.
  • Diffusion image models learn to reverse a gradual noising process, conditioned on text.
  • Training is mostly self-supervised: the next token is the label, so no manual labelling is needed.
  • One model is general purpose (summarize, translate, code) and is steered by natural-language prompts.
  • Contrast: generation versus discrimination (predicting a label).
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Is the model copying its training data?
  • Why does the same prompt sometimes give different answers?
💡 Tip: Definition first, example second. That pair is the winning pattern.
📚 References
  • Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (GPT-3)
Q2How is Generative AI different from traditional AI?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Discriminative versus generative framing
  • You can name differences in objective, output, data and evaluation
  • Awareness that real systems combine both
🗣️ Say this

Traditional or discriminative AI makes decisions about existing data, like spam or not spam, churn or no churn, and returns a label or a number. Generative AI learns the data distribution itself and produces open-ended new content such as an essay, an image or a SQL query. Traditional models need labelled, task-specific data and do one job. Generative models are pretrained on huge unlabelled corpora and switch tasks through the prompt. In production they often work together: a classifier routes a support ticket and a generative model drafts the reply.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Objective: discriminative models learn P(label | input), a decision boundary; generative models learn P(data) so they can sample.
  • Output space: a fixed set of classes or a number versus open-ended sequences.
  • Evaluation: accuracy, precision and recall for classifiers; human preference, ROUGE/BLEU, LLM-as-a-judge and factuality checks for generation.
  • Interface: traditional models sit behind engineer-built APIs; generative models can be steered by non-technical users in plain language.
  • Mental model: 'what is this?' versus 'create this for me'.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Give an example where you would still prefer a classic classifier.
  • How would you evaluate a generative feature?
💡 Tip: Mention a hybrid example (classifier routes, LLM drafts) to show production thinking.
Q3What are some real-world applications of Generative AI?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Applications grouped by domain, not a random list
  • One concrete product or business outcome per domain
  • Ability to tailor the answer to the interviewer's industry
🗣️ Say this

I group them by domain. In software engineering: code completion, review and test generation with tools like Copilot or Claude Code. In customer experience: support agents that resolve tickets and summarize long threads. In content and marketing: ad copy, personalized emails, image and video generation. In education: adaptive tutors and quiz generation. In healthcare: clinical note summarization and drug-molecule design. In finance and legal: contract analysis and compliance Q&A. The biggest enterprise pattern is a knowledge assistant: an LLM plus retrieval over private data plus tool calling, so it can act, not just answer.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Enterprise knowledge assistant = LLM + RAG over company data + tools; this pattern sits under CRM, banking and developer copilots.
  • Healthcare and finance use cases need stronger guardrails, audit logs and human review.
  • Agentic workflow automation extends chat into doing multi-step tasks.
  • Value is measured in business outcomes: tickets resolved, hours saved, conversion lift.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Which of these would you build first for a bank, and why?
  • What could go wrong in the healthcare example?
💡 Tip: If you know the interviewer's industry, lead with that industry's use cases.
Q4What is a Large Language Model (LLM)?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Transformer plus next-token prediction
  • 'Large' explained along parameters, data and compute
  • Awareness of emergent abilities and the alignment layer
🗣️ Say this

An LLM is a deep neural network, almost always a Transformer, trained on huge text corpora to predict the next token. Large means three things: billions to trillions of parameters, trillions of training tokens, and thousands of GPUs for weeks. This one simple objective, at enough scale, produces emergent abilities such as grammar, world knowledge, reasoning, translation and code, none of which were explicitly programmed. On top of the base model, instruction tuning and RLHF make it behave as a helpful assistant. Examples are GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama and Mistral.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Architecture: decoder-only Transformer with stacked self-attention and feed-forward layers.
  • Parameters are the learned weights; e.g. Llama 3 ships in 8B and 70B sizes.
  • Context window is the maximum number of tokens attended at once, from about 8K to over 1M.
  • The tokenizer maps text to integer IDs; the vocabulary is part of the model's interface.
  • Multimodal LLMs also accept images, audio and video.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • What does 'emergent' mean here, and is it real or a measurement artifact?
  • What is the difference between a base model and a chat model?
📚 References
  • The Illustrated GPT-2
Q5What are the major stages in the LLM lifecycle?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • You know the full pipeline, not just the API call
  • You can say where application teams enter
  • You connect stages to behaviours like cutoffs and hallucination
🗣️ Say this

There are seven stages. One, data collection and preparation: crawl, deduplicate, filter and tokenize trillions of tokens. Two, pretraining: next-token prediction that produces a base model. Three, supervised fine-tuning on instruction and response pairs. Four, alignment with RLHF, RLAIF or DPO so outputs are helpful and safe. Five, evaluation and red-teaming. Six, deployment and inference with quantization, KV caching and batching. Seven, monitoring for quality, drift, cost and misuse. Most application teams join at stage six by consuming a hosted model.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Pretraining dominates compute; later stages are far cheaper (illustrative in the lab, not measured).
  • Teams may loop back to stages 3-4 by fine-tuning on their own data.
  • Knowledge cutoffs come from stage 1-2; style and refusals come from stages 3-4.
  • Evaluation uses benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval, GSM8K) plus domain evals and adversarial probing.
  • Monitoring feeds the next training cycle or fine-tune.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Where does RAG fit in this lifecycle?
  • Which stage causes hallucinations?
💡 Tip: Say which stage your team operates at; it shows you know the boundaries.
Q6What is pre-training in an LLM?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Self-supervised next-token prediction
  • Why it is the most expensive phase
  • Base model versus assistant distinction
🗣️ Say this

Pretraining is the first and most expensive phase, where a randomly initialised Transformer is trained on a massive unlabelled corpus. For decoder-only models the objective is causal language modeling: given tokens 1 to n, predict token n+1, and minimise cross-entropy loss with gradient descent. It is self-supervised because the next token is the label. The result is a base model that is great at continuing text but does not follow instructions, so asked for the capital of France it may just produce more questions. Instruction tuning fixes that in the next stage.

🔍 Go deeper
  • It learns syntax, world knowledge up to the cutoff, reasoning patterns and internal embeddings.
  • It consumes the large majority of training compute, often tens of millions of dollars of GPU time.
  • Scaling laws (Kaplan et al.; Chinchilla) show loss falls predictably as parameters, data and compute scale together.
  • Chinchilla suggested many models were under-trained for their size, and that data should scale with parameters.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • What is cross-entropy loss intuitively?
  • Why not just train bigger forever?
📚 References
  • Training Compute-Optimal LLMs (Chinchilla)
  • Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models (Kaplan et al.)
Q7What is fine-tuning in an LLM?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Continued training on a smaller targeted dataset
  • Knows the types, especially PEFT
  • Fine-tuning for behaviour, RAG for facts
🗣️ Say this

Fine-tuning continues training a pretrained model on a smaller, targeted dataset to adapt its behaviour, style or domain skill. Because the model already knows language, thousands of examples can be enough instead of trillions of tokens. Common types are supervised or instruction tuning, domain-adaptive tuning, task-specific tuning, preference tuning like RLHF or DPO, and parameter-efficient methods like LoRA. Fine-tuning changes how the model behaves much more reliably than it adds new facts, so a classic pattern is fine-tune for format and tone, use RAG for facts.

🔍 Go deeper
  • SFT trains on (prompt, ideal response) pairs and turns a base model into a chat assistant.
  • PEFT such as LoRA and QLoRA trains tiny adapter matrices rather than all weights and is the practical default.
  • Fresh or proprietary knowledge is better injected with RAG; the two are complementary.
  • Risks: catastrophic forgetting, overfitting on small data, cost of maintaining a custom model.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • When would you fine-tune instead of using RAG?
  • What is LoRA in one sentence?
Q8What happens during inference?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Weights are frozen; no learning happens
  • Prefill versus decode distinction
  • Sampling controls and why output tokens cost more
🗣️ Say this

Inference is using a trained model; the weights are frozen and it only runs forward passes. The text is tokenized, then prefill processes the whole prompt in one parallel pass and builds the key-value cache, which dominates time to first token. Then the decoding loop starts: the model outputs a probability distribution over the vocabulary, one token is chosen, appended, and the loop repeats, which is why answers stream word by word. Sampling uses greedy, temperature, top-k or top-p. Generation stops at an end token, a stop string or the max-token limit, and tokens are detokenized to text.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Low temperature gives deterministic and factual output; high gives diverse and creative output.
  • KV caching avoids recomputing attention for earlier tokens; continuous batching packs many users onto one GPU.
  • Quantization to 8-bit or 4-bit shrinks memory; speculative decoding uses a small draft model verified by the large one.
  • Input tokens are cheap parallel prefill; output tokens are sequential decode, hence different prices.
  • Latency scales roughly linearly with output length.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Why is the first token slower than later ones?
  • What does temperature 0 do?
💡 Tip: Name prefill and decode explicitly; it signals real serving knowledge.
Q9What is a token in LLMs?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Subword unit, not a word or character
  • Knows BPE-style tokenizers and rules of thumb
  • Understands uneven cost across languages and code
🗣️ Say this

A token is the unit of text a model actually reads and writes. Tokens are usually subword pieces made by a tokenizer such as Byte-Pair Encoding, which builds a vocabulary of 32K to 200K entries by repeatedly merging the most frequent character pairs. Common words become one token, while rare words split, so tokenization might become token plus ization. Each token maps to an integer ID and then to an embedding vector. In English, one token is about four characters or three quarters of a word. Tokens are the currency of LLMs: context, latency and cost are all measured in them.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Code, URLs and non-Latin scripts, including many Indian languages, can cost 2-5x more tokens for the same content.
  • Numbers often split oddly, which partly explains weak arithmetic in older models.
  • WordPiece and SentencePiece are alternatives to BPE.
  • Special tokens mark sequence boundaries, roles and tool calls.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Roughly how many tokens is a 3,000-word document?
  • Why do some languages cost more to use?
📚 References
  • OpenAI Tokenizer (interactive)
  • Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units (BPE)
Q10Why are tokens important for language models?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Cost, context, latency and quality all in tokens
  • Budgeting mindset for prompts and RAG
  • Special tokens implement chat and tool formats
🗣️ Say this

Every constraint in an LLM system is counted in tokens. The context window, such as 128K tokens, must hold the system prompt, history, retrieved documents and the answer together, so chunk sizes and truncation are token-budget decisions. Cost is billed per million input and output tokens, so a verbose system prompt repeated on every call quietly multiplies your bill. Latency grows with output tokens because they are generated one at a time. Tokenization even affects quality, since poorly tokenized languages give the model less effective context.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • 128K tokens is roughly 96K English words.
  • Three production dashboards are token-based: cost per conversation, tokens per second, and context-window utilisation.
  • Special tokens (begin/end, role separators, tool-call markers) physically implement chat formatting and function calling.
  • Asking for concise answers is a genuine latency and cost optimisation.
  • Multilingual and code models train custom tokenizers for efficiency.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How would you cut the token cost of a RAG chatbot?
  • What happens when the prompt exceeds the context window?
Q11What are embeddings?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Dense vectors representing meaning
  • Semantic geometry: similar means close
  • Contextual versus static embeddings
🗣️ Say this

An embedding is a dense vector, typically 384 to 3,072 numbers, that represents the meaning of a word, sentence, document or image as a point in space. Similar meanings land near each other, so how do I reset my password and I forgot my login credentials sit close together even with almost no shared words. They come from neural networks trained contrastively so related items attract and unrelated ones repel. Modern contextual embeddings from Transformer encoders handle context, so bank near river differs from bank near loan, whereas Word2Vec gave one fixed vector per word.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Fixed dimensionality regardless of input length.
  • Similarity is computed with cosine similarity or dot product.
  • Classic word vectors showed analogies: king - man + woman is about queen.
  • Multimodal models like CLIP put images and text in one space.
  • Inside an LLM the first layer turns each token ID into an embedding and every layer refines them.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How do you measure the similarity of two embeddings?
  • What changes if you switch embedding models?
📚 References
  • Embedding Projector (TensorFlow)
  • Sentence-BERT
Q12Why are embeddings used in AI systems?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Turn meaning into computable math
  • Semantic search and RAG as the key use
  • Practical reasons: cheap, fast, multilingual
🗣️ Say this

Embeddings turn unstructured content into vectors so we can compute similarity, search, cluster and classify. The most important use is semantic search and RAG: embed documents once, embed the query at runtime, and fetch the nearest vectors. They also power recommendations, clustering and deduplication, intent classification, anomaly detection, agent memory and semantic caches. They dominate because they are cheap compared with an LLM call, fast thanks to approximate nearest-neighbour indexes that search millions of items in milliseconds, and language-agnostic, so Hindi and English sentences with the same meaning sit close together.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Recommendation: users and items share a space; recommend items near the user vector.
  • Anomalies are points far from every known cluster.
  • A lightweight classifier on embeddings often beats keyword rules for routing.
  • Semantic caches match new queries to earlier answered ones to cut LLM costs.
  • Agent memory retrieves relevant history semantically rather than by exact match.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Where do embeddings fail in search?
  • How would you build a semantic cache?
Q13What is the Transformer architecture?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Self-attention replacing recurrence, parallel training
  • The core components in order
  • Encoder, decoder and encoder-decoder variants
🗣️ Say this

The Transformer, from the 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need, replaced recurrence with self-attention so every token can look at every other token in parallel, which made massive GPU training possible. Tokens become embeddings plus positional information, since attention itself ignores order. Multi-head self-attention computes queries, keys and values and weights tokens by softmax of Q times K over root d. A feed-forward network per token adds capacity, residual connections and layer norm stabilise deep stacks, and a final linear layer with softmax predicts the next token. GPT-style models are decoder-only.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Positional methods: sinusoidal, learned, or RoPE in modern models.
  • Multiple heads learn different relations such as syntax, coreference and long-range links.
  • The FFN holds most parameters and is widely believed to store factual knowledge.
  • Encoder-only (BERT) suits understanding and embeddings; decoder-only (GPT, Llama, Claude) uses causal masking for generation; encoder-decoder (T5) suits translation.
  • Refinements: GQA, FlashAttention and Mixture-of-Experts, which activates only a fraction of parameters per token.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Why divide by the square root of d?
  • What does causal masking do?
📚 References
  • Attention Is All You Need
  • The Illustrated Transformer
Q14What problem does the attention mechanism solve?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Long-range dependencies and RNN bottleneck
  • Parallelism as the second win
  • Quadratic cost as the honest trade-off
🗣️ Say this

Attention solves the long-range dependency problem: while processing one word, the model can directly use information from any other word, however far away. RNNs and LSTMs read one token at a time and squeezed everything into one fixed hidden state, so details from word 5 were mostly lost by word 500, and they could not be parallelised. With attention, each token takes a weighted mix of all others, with weights computed from query-key similarity. In the trophy and suitcase sentence, it can attend straight to trophy. The cost is quadratic in sequence length.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Weights are content-based and learned, not fixed positional rules.
  • All scores are matrix multiplications, ideal for GPUs and training on trillions of tokens.
  • Attention maps give partial interpretability, though not a full explanation.
  • Quadratic compute and memory motivates FlashAttention, sliding-window, sparse and linear attention.
  • Vanishing gradients explain RNN information decay.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Why does long context get expensive?
  • Are attention maps a faithful explanation of model behaviour?
💡 Tip: Use the trophy and suitcase sentence; it makes the idea concrete.
📚 References
  • Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate (Bahdanau et al.)
  • FlashAttention
Q15Name some popular LLM providers and models.
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • You follow the ecosystem
  • Closed versus open-weight organisation
  • Trade-off reasoning, not just names
🗣️ Say this

I split them into closed API providers and open-weight families. Closed: OpenAI with GPT-4o, GPT-4.1 and the o-series reasoning models; Anthropic with Claude in Opus, Sonnet and Haiku tiers; Google with Gemini, plus Gemma as its open sibling; and others like Cohere, xAI and Amazon Nova. 🔍 Go deeper

  • DeepSeek-R1 is notable for open reasoning capability.
  • Self-hosting tools: Ollama for local, vLLM for high-throughput serving.
  • Model names and versions change fast; check current docs before committing.

  • What would you test before switching providers?

Q1What is prompt engineering?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you see the prompt as the program, not just a casual question?
  • Can you name the components of a production prompt?
  • Signal: you describe it as an empirical loop with test sets and versioning.
  • Red flag: 'magic words' with no measurement.
🗣️ Say this

Prompt engineering is designing, structuring and iteratively refining the input to an LLM so it reliably produces the output you want. Because the model's behaviour is conditioned entirely on its context, the prompt is effectively the program, written in natural language. A solid prompt has a role, a precise task, context to ground the answer, a few examples, an output format and constraints such as 'say I don't know if the context lacks the answer'. Crucially it is empirical: I write a prompt, run it on a representative test set, measure failures, and iterate, keeping prompts in version control with regression evals just like code.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Spectrum: from clearly phrasing a question to multi-part templates with variables, schemas, guardrails and examples.
  • Components: role/persona, task instruction (including what not to do), context, few-shot examples, output format, constraints.
  • Prompts are templates: dynamic variables are filled at runtime with retrieved documents, user metadata and the user's words.
  • Mature teams keep a prompt repository, review changes, and gate releases on eval scores.
  • It sits before RAG and fine-tuning on the escalation ladder because iteration takes minutes, not weeks.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How would you know that a new version of a prompt is actually better?
  • Which part of a prompt would you change first if the output format was inconsistent?
💡 Tip: Say 'the prompt is the program' and 'I evaluate prompts like code' within your first two sentences.
📚 References
  • OpenAI: Prompt engineering guide
Q2Why is prompt engineering important?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you justify it with business and engineering reasons, not hype?
  • Do you know it is the cheapest lever before RAG or fine-tuning?
  • Signal: you connect it to reliability, safety, cost and portability.
🗣️ Say this

Because the same model can perform badly or brilliantly depending only on the prompt, sometimes swinging accuracy by tens of points on reasoning tasks. It is also the cheapest lever: improving a prompt takes minutes, while fine-tuning needs data, GPUs and MLOps, so you should exhaust prompting and RAG first. In production, good prompts cut hallucinations by demanding grounding, force parseable output schemas, and add guardrails. They also control token cost and latency, and make it easier to switch models. My line is that the weights set the capability ceiling, and the prompt decides how much of it you reach.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Quality variance: structure, examples or 'think step by step' can change benchmark accuracy dramatically with no training.
  • Cost of change: prompt edit is minutes; fine-tuning needs data collection, training runs and serving a new model.
  • Reliability: grounding instructions reduce hallucination, schemas make output machine-parseable, guardrails resist injection and drift.
  • Cost and latency: shorter prompts and capped outputs reduce spend directly, and cached static prefixes make it cheaper still.
  • Portability: a well-structured, evaluated prompt moves between GPT, Claude and Llama with far less rework.
  • Access: domain experts can encode behaviour without writing code.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • When is prompt engineering NOT enough?
  • Give an example where a production failure was actually a prompt bug.
💡 Tip: Close with: 'In most failures I debug, the model was capable and the prompt was the bug.'
Q3What is a system prompt?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you know it is developer-controlled and sits above user turns?
  • Can you list what belongs in it?
  • Signal: you mention that the hierarchy comes from training, so it is not absolute.
  • Signal: you know it is re-sent every request and can be cached.
🗣️ Say this

A system prompt is the privileged, developer-written instruction block placed at the top of the conversation, before any user message. It defines who the model is, which rules it follows and how it behaves for the whole session, and end users normally never see it. Typical contents are identity and role, tone and formatting rules, scope and refusal policy, tool descriptions, and persistent facts like today's date or company policy. Models are instruction-tuned to give it more authority than user text, which makes guardrails meaningful, though not bulletproof. It is sent with every request because the model is stateless, so its length is a recurring cost that prompt caching can reduce.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • API form: the 'system' parameter in Anthropic's Messages API, or a message with role 'system' (or 'developer') in OpenAI APIs.
  • Content categories: identity, behavioural rules, scope and guardrails, tools and when to use them, persistent context.
  • Higher authority is learned during instruction tuning, not enforced by architecture; prompt injection attacks target exactly this gap.
  • Statelessness means the system prompt tokens are billed on every call, so keep it tight and keep the static part at the front for cache hits.
  • Never put real secrets in it; assume it can be extracted.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Can a user extract or override the system prompt? How would you defend?

Q4What is a user prompt?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you understand it is per-turn and untrusted?
  • Do you know real apps wrap raw input in a template?
  • Signal: you mention that the full history is resent on every call.
🗣️ Say this

A user prompt is the message sent with the user role: the actual question or content for the current turn, from the end user or from the application on their behalf. Where the system prompt sets long-lived rules, the user prompt carries the immediate task, such as 'summarise this contract'. It changes on every request and must be treated as untrusted, since it can contain mistakes or deliberate prompt-injection attempts. In real applications it is usually wrapped in a template that injects retrieved documents, metadata and format reminders. In a multi-turn chat the whole alternating history is resent each call, since the model has no memory of its own.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Dynamic and turn-specific, in contrast to the mostly static system prompt.
  • Untrusted by default: assume it may try to override instructions; defence is layered (delimiters, input filtering, output validation).
  • Templating: the app builds the final user message from the user's words plus retrieved context, user metadata and format instructions.
  • Multimodal: modern APIs accept text plus images, PDFs or audio in the user message.
  • The latest user turn is what the model primarily answers, but it conditions on the entire history that is resent.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How do you keep user-supplied text from being interpreted as instructions?
  • What happens to cost as a conversation gets longer?

Q5What is the difference between a system prompt and a user prompt?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you compare them on authority, author, lifetime, content and trust?
  • Do you know the hierarchy is learned, not architectural?
  • Red flag: claiming the system prompt is 'secure' or cannot be overridden.
🗣️ Say this

Think of the system prompt as the employee handbook and the user prompt as the customer's request walking in. The developer writes the system prompt; it persists across the conversation, holds identity, rules, tools and guardrails, is hidden and trusted, and models are trained to prioritise it. The user prompt comes from the end user at runtime, is per-turn, holds the task and data, is visible and untrusted, and is the main attack surface for prompt injection. If the system says 'English only' and the user asks for French, an aligned model follows the system rule. That priority is learned in training, not hardware-enforced, so I add input filtering, output validation and tool permissions.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Authority: system instructions win on conflict, a behaviour reinforced through instruction tuning and work on an 'instruction hierarchy'.
  • Author and lifetime: developer vs end user; whole session vs single turn.
  • Trust: system is trusted and hidden; user is untrusted and the main injection vector.
  • Engineering treatment: system prompts are versioned, evaluated and cached; user prompts are sanitised and templated.
  • No architectural separation exists: both are tokens in one context, so defence in depth is required.
  • Retrieved documents and tool outputs are also untrusted data and should be delimited, not treated as instructions.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • A user types 'ignore all previous instructions'. Walk me through what defences stop this.
  • Where would you put few-shot examples, system or user turn, and why?
💡 Tip: Volunteer the nuance that the hierarchy is trained behaviour, not a security boundary.

Q6What is zero-shot prompting?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you define it precisely (instructions only, no examples)?
  • Do you know when it is enough and where it fails?
  • Signal: you treat it as the baseline to beat.
🗣️ Say this

Zero-shot prompting means asking the model to do a task with instructions only, with no examples of the task in the prompt. The model relies on what it learned in pre-training and instruction tuning. For example, 'Classify the sentiment of this review as Positive, Negative or Neutral' followed by the review. It works well for common, well-defined tasks like summarisation, translation and sentiment, especially on strong frontier models, and when tokens and latency must be minimal. It struggles when the output format must be exact, when labels or conventions are company-specific, and on edge cases. My workflow is always to try zero-shot first as the baseline.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Works for tasks abundant in training data: summarisation, translation, sentiment, general Q&A, standard code.
  • Frontier instruction-tuned models are strong zero-shot performers.
  • Failure mode 1: unspecified format, so casing, label spelling or JSON shape drifts between calls.
  • Failure mode 2: niche taxonomies and styles that instructions alone cannot convey.
  • Failure mode 3: edge cases and ambiguous inputs where an example would pin down behaviour.
  • Escalate to few-shot only when evals show a gap, not by default.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How do you decide it is time to add examples?
  • Does zero-shot CoT count as zero-shot?
💡 Tip: Call it 'the baseline' and say you escalate based on eval results.
Q7What is few-shot prompting?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you know it is in-context learning with no weight updates?
  • Can you give best practices for choosing examples?
  • Red flag: confusing few-shot prompting with fine-tuning.
🗣️ Say this

Few-shot prompting puts a small number of worked examples, typically two to ten input-output pairs, in the prompt before the real input. The model spots the pattern and applies it to the new input, which is called in-context learning and was shown in the GPT-3 paper. No weights change; it is pattern matching inside the context window at inference time, which is the key difference from fine-tuning. For extraction, I would show two orders converted to JSON and then give a third order. Good examples are diverse, cover edge cases, are perfectly consistent in format, and are balanced across classes. Every example costs tokens on every call.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • In-context learning: the model infers the task from demonstrations; nothing is stored after the call.
  • Consistency matters because the model copies surface patterns, including label spelling and JSON shape.
  • Ordering can matter; placing the most representative example last exploits recency.
  • Balance classes so the model is not biased towards the majority label.
  • Cost: static examples are paid for on every request; dynamic few-shot retrieves the most similar examples from a vector store per query.
  • Include at least one tricky or ambiguous case showing the desired fallback, such as UNKNOWN.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How many examples would you start with, and how do you know when to stop?
  • What is dynamic few-shot selection and when is it worth the complexity?
💡 Tip: State 'no weights are updated' early; it is the distinction interviewers listen for.
📚 References
  • Brown et al. 2020: Language Models are Few-Shot Learners
Q8What is Chain-of-Thought prompting?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you explain why it works mechanically (one token at a time)?
  • Do you know the variants and the cost trade-off?
  • Signal: you warn the visible reasoning may not be faithful.
🗣️ Say this

Chain-of-Thought prompting elicits intermediate reasoning steps before the final answer, which improves math, logic and multi-step tasks. It was introduced by Wei et al. in 2022. It works because the model generates one token at a time, so writing the steps out gives it space to think and lets each step condition on the previous ones, instead of leaping to the answer in one pass. Take a juggler with 16 balls, half golf balls, half of those blue: stepping through gives 16, then 8, then 4. The cost is more output tokens and latency, and the shown reasoning is not always faithful, so I use it for reasoning-heavy tasks only.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Zero-shot CoT: append 'Let's think step by step' (Kojima et al.); few-shot CoT: examples that include worked reasoning.
  • Self-consistency: sample several chains and majority-vote the final answer, trading cost for accuracy.
  • Tree of Thoughts and ReAct extend it with branching exploration or interleaved tool use.
  • Reasoning models (OpenAI o-series, DeepSeek-R1, Claude extended thinking) internalise CoT through RL and produce long reasoning traces natively.
  • Trade-offs: more output tokens, higher latency, and the stated reasoning can differ from the real internal computation.
  • Skip it for simple lookups and classification where it only adds cost.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How does self-consistency improve accuracy and what does it cost?
  • Do you still need 'think step by step' with a reasoning model?
💡 Tip: Use the juggler example; it is short and shows the decomposition concretely.

  • Wei et al. 2022: Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning
  • Kojima et al. 2022: Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Reasoners
Q9When would you use few-shot prompting instead of zero-shot prompting?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you default to zero-shot and escalate on evidence?
  • Can you list concrete triggers: strict format, custom taxonomy, style, edge cases?
  • Signal: you know when to stay zero-shot and when few-shot is the signal to fine-tune.
🗣️ Say this

I default to zero-shot and move to few-shot when the model needs to be shown rather than told. The triggers are a strict output structure that code must parse, a company-specific task like an internal ticket taxonomy, a precise style or tone to match, edge cases that need pinned-down behaviour, inconsistent results in zero-shot evals, and smaller models that benefit from examples more. I stay zero-shot when the task is common and already solved, when context budget is precious, or when examples would reduce creativity. In practice: zero-shot, measure, add two or three examples for the failures, measure again. If that still fails at scale, consider fine-tuning.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • One example can replace a paragraph of format instructions, especially for JSON schemas, fixed labels or CSV rows.
  • Proprietary taxonomies and house styles cannot be inferred from instructions alone.
  • Examples show boundary decisions, such as 'ambiguous input -> UNKNOWN', more reliably than prose.
  • Small open models (around 7B) gain far more from few-shot than frontier models do.
  • Costs: extra tokens per call, less room for RAG context, and risk of anchoring on the examples in creative tasks.
  • Dynamic example retrieval lowers the token cost while keeping relevance.

  • How do you pick which examples to include?

Q10What are some common prompt optimization techniques?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you organise techniques into structural, reasoning and engineering groups?
  • Do you mention evaluation-driven iteration?
  • Signal: you can narrate a live prompt-fix checklist.
🗣️ Say this

I group them in three buckets. Structural: assign a role, decompose into numbered steps or chained prompts, separate instructions from data with XML tags or delimiters, specify an output schema or use structured outputs, and phrase instructions positively. Reasoning and quality: chain-of-thought for reasoning tasks, few-shot examples, self-critique, grounding instructions like 'answer only from the context and cite it', and explicitly allowing 'I don't know'. Engineering: low temperature around 0 to 0.3 for factual work, deliberate max_tokens, prompt caching for static prefixes, and evaluation-driven iteration with versioned prompts. Frameworks like DSPy can even optimise prompts automatically.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Structural: role, task decomposition and prompt chaining, delimiters (also an injection defence), schemas or JSON mode, do-instructions over don't-instructions.
  • Reasoning: CoT with the answer after the reasoning, few-shot, draft-critique-finalise loops, grounding for RAG, giving the model an out.
  • Engineering: temperature 0-0.3 for factual or structured tasks and 0.7-1.0 for creative; set max_tokens and stop sequences.
  • Caching: keep system prompt, examples and documents identical and first so the provider can reuse the prefix.
  • Evaluation: test set plus exact match, rubric or LLM-as-judge scoring; change one thing at a time.
  • Automation: DSPy-style frameworks search for better instructions and examples against a metric.

Q1What is an embedding vector?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you define it as a fixed-length dense array that encodes meaning geometrically, not as 'a list of numbers'?
  • Do you know similar meaning means nearby vectors, and that individual dimensions are not interpretable?
  • Red flag: forgetting that vectors from different models are incompatible.
🗣️ Say this

An embedding is a fixed-length array of floats, for example 384, 768, 1,536 or 3,072 numbers, that represents the meaning of a piece of content as a point in a high-dimensional space. No single dimension means anything on its own; meaning lives in the geometry. A good model places similar inputs close together, so 'how do I cancel my subscription' lands near 'I want to stop my monthly plan' and far from a recipe. Because a short query and a long paragraph give the same length vector, they can be compared directly with math.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Dense, not sparse: unlike one-hot or TF-IDF vectors, which are vocabulary-sized and mostly zeros, every dimension carries signal.
  • Comparable by cosine similarity, dot product or Euclidean distance; this is the core operation of vector search.
  • Model-specific space: vectors from different models are incompatible, so queries and documents must use the same model, and switching models means re-embedding everything.
  • Storage maths: 1,536 dims x 4 bytes is about 6 KB per chunk, so one million chunks is roughly 6 GB of raw vectors before index overhead.
  • That scale is exactly what vector databases are designed to index and search.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Why can't you compare vectors from two different embedding models?
  • How much memory does a million 1,536-dimension float32 vectors need?
💡 Tip: Quote the storage arithmetic; it shows you think about production, not just concepts.

Q2How are embeddings generated?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you know the pipeline: tokenize, encode, pool, normalize?
  • Do you know contrastive training and why hard negatives matter?
  • Do you state the operational rule: same model for documents and queries?
🗣️ Say this

Text goes through a trained Transformer encoder. We tokenize it, run it through the encoder so every token gets a context-aware vector, pool those into one vector with mean pooling or the CLS token, and usually L2-normalize so cosine becomes a dot product. The models are trained contrastively: given pairs that should match, like a question and its answer, the loss pulls positives together and pushes negatives apart. In practice you call an API such as OpenAI or Cohere, or run an open model like BGE or E5 locally. The key rule is to use the same model at indexing and query time.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Contrastive losses: InfoNCE or triplet; hard negatives (passages that look relevant but are not) teach fine-grained discrimination.
  • Typical recipe: pre-train on billions of weakly paired web texts, then fine-tune on curated retrieval data such as MS MARCO and NQ; this is how BGE, E5 and GTE rank high on MTEB.
  • Multimodal models like CLIP train an image encoder and a text encoder jointly so an image and its caption share one space.
  • Matryoshka-style embeddings (supported by OpenAI text-embedding-3) can be truncated to fewer dimensions for cheaper storage with modest quality loss.
  • Local open models (sentence-transformers, BGE-M3) are chosen for data privacy and cost; APIs for convenience.
  • Swapping the embedding model without re-indexing silently breaks retrieval.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • What is the difference between mean pooling and CLS pooling?
  • Why does hard-negative mining improve retrieval?
  • What happens if you upgrade the embedding model but keep the old index?
💡 Tip: Mention the normalize step: it explains why dot product equals cosine later.

Q3Why are embeddings useful for semantic search?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you explain that search becomes geometry: embed, then find nearest?
  • Do you give a concrete vocabulary-mismatch example?
  • Do you honestly mention the weakness with exact identifiers?
🗣️ Say this

Embeddings make meaning computable. You embed the documents once offline, embed the query at search time, and return the documents whose vectors are nearest. That fixes vocabulary mismatch: 'my laptop won't switch on' finds 'Troubleshooting power failures in notebooks' with no shared keywords. You also get synonyms, paraphrases, typo tolerance and cross-lingual search from one index. The honest limit is exact identifiers like part numbers or error codes, which is why production systems add keyword search in a hybrid setup.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • No hand-maintained synonym lists, stemming rules or query expansion.
  • Cross-lingual: a multilingual model places a Hindi query near relevant English documents.
  • Question-to-answer matching: models trained on QA pairs embed questions near passages that answer them, which is what RAG needs.
  • Efficiency: documents are embedded once; at query time only the query is embedded, then an approximate nearest neighbour lookup takes milliseconds over millions of vectors.
  • Weakness: pure semantic search can miss exact tokens such as SKUs, error codes and names, so use hybrid search.
↪ Likely follow-ups

  • How does a multilingual embedding model enable cross-language retrieval?
Q4What is similarity search?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you define k-nearest neighbours with its inputs: query vector, collection, metric, k?
  • Do you distinguish exact kNN from approximate (ANN) search and know when each fits?
  • Do you connect it to RAG retrieval and debugging?
🗣️ Say this

Similarity search finds the k stored vectors closest to a query vector under a chosen metric: cosine, dot product or Euclidean. Exact kNN compares the query against every vector, which is perfectly accurate but costs N times d operations, fine up to a few hundred thousand vectors. For millions or billions, approximate nearest neighbour indexes such as HNSW, IVF and product quantization trade a little recall for orders of magnitude of speed. In RAG, retrieval is exactly this: embed the question, fetch the top-k chunks, pass them to the LLM.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Output is the top-k items with scores, optionally pre-filtered by metadata like tenant_id, date or document type.
  • HNSW: layered navigable small-world graph, the de facto standard. IVF: cluster the space and search only nearby clusters. PQ: compress vectors to save memory.
  • Typical numbers: HNSW searches 10 million vectors in single-digit milliseconds at 95 to 99 percent recall.
  • Tuning knobs such as efSearch and nprobe slide you along the speed versus recall curve.
  • When retrieval is poor, suspect in order: the embedding model, the chunking, then k and the metric.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • What is recall in the context of ANN indexes?
  • What does efSearch change?
  • When would you still choose exact search?
Q5What is cosine similarity?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you state the formula and interpret +1, 0 and -1?
  • Do you know why direction rather than magnitude matters for text?
  • Do you know the normalized-vector shortcut and that thresholds must be calibrated?
🗣️ Say this

Cosine similarity is the cosine of the angle between two vectors: the dot product divided by the product of their lengths. It is 1 when they point the same way, 0 when orthogonal, and -1 when opposite. It ignores magnitude, so a tweet and an essay on the same topic still match. For example, A = [1,2,3] and B = [2,4,6] have cosine exactly 1 even though their Euclidean distance is large. If vectors are L2-normalized, cosine equals a plain dot product, which is the cheapest operation.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Formula: cos(theta) = (A . B) / (||A|| x ||B||).
  • Length can correlate with text length or token frequency, which is noise for meaning; cosine removes it.
  • Bounded in [-1, 1], so easy to threshold and compare across queries.
  • Real text embeddings rarely go negative; unrelated text often still scores well above 0, so thresholds like 0.8 are model-specific and must be calibrated on your own data.
  • Alternatives: Euclidean (L2) distance, unnormalized dot product (when magnitude carries signal, as in some recommenders) and Manhattan (L1).
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • When would dot product be better than cosine?
  • If vectors are normalized, how do cosine and Euclidean distance relate?
💡 Tip: Do the [1,2,3] vs [2,4,6] example out loud; it is short and memorable.
Q6What is the difference between keyword search and semantic search?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you describe both mechanisms (inverted index with BM25 versus embeddings)?
  • Can you give a failure case for each?
  • Do you land on hybrid search as the production answer?
🗣️ Say this

Keyword search, like BM25 in Elasticsearch, uses an inverted index and scores by term frequency, inverse document frequency and length. It is excellent for exact identifiers, error codes and names, and it is explainable, but it misses synonyms and paraphrases. Semantic search embeds the query and documents and ranks by similarity, so it finds 'Guidelines for ending staff agreements' for 'terminate an employee contract', but it can wander on 'invoice INV-2024-0832'. Production systems run both, fuse the results with Reciprocal Rank Fusion or weighted scores, and often rerank.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Keyword weaknesses: vocabulary mismatch, no cross-lingual ability, brittle to typos, no sense of intent.
  • Semantic weaknesses: blurs exact tokens, needs embedding infrastructure, re-indexing on model change, and scores are less explainable.
  • Hybrid = BM25 and vector search in parallel, fusion (RRF or weighted), optional cross-encoder rerank.
  • Hybrid gets precision on exact terms plus recall on meaning.

Q7What is a vector database?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you separate it from a plain index library and from a relational DB?
  • Do you list core capabilities: ANN, metadata filtering, CRUD, hybrid, scaling?
  • Can you name selection criteria rather than just brands?
🗣️ Say this

A vector database stores embeddings together with their metadata and answers top-k nearest-neighbour queries at scale with low latency. Where a relational database is built around exact matches and range scans, a vector database is built around ANN indexes like HNSW, IVF and PQ. It also supports metadata filtering, such as tenant and date, upserts and deletes as documents change, often hybrid search, plus sharding, replication and backups. In RAG it is the knowledge store: ingestion writes chunk vectors, every query reads the top-k.

🔍 Go deeper

  • Landscape: managed or dedicated (Pinecone, Weaviate Cloud, Qdrant Cloud, Zilliz/Milvus, Chroma); libraries (FAISS, HNSWlib, Annoy); extensions (pgvector, Elasticsearch/OpenSearch k-NN, Redis, MongoDB Atlas Vector Search).
  • Metadata filtering is essential for multi-tenant SaaS and access control.
  • Selection criteria: scale, latency SLO, filtering needs, hybrid support, managed versus self-hosted, multi-tenancy and cost.

Q8Why can't traditional relational databases efficiently handle vector search?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you explain why B-trees and hash indexes cannot prune high-dimensional proximity?
  • Do you know the curse of dimensionality and the O(N x d) full-scan cost?
  • Do you give the nuance that pgvector bridges the gap at small to mid scale?
🗣️ Say this

Relational indexes like B-trees accelerate equality and range queries on ordered scalar values, but there is no ordering of points in 1,536 dimensions that preserves proximity, so they cannot prune the search. Classical spatial indexes like KD-trees degrade to near-linear scans beyond roughly 10 to 20 dimensions. Without a specialised index every query computes a distance to every row: at 10 million vectors and 1,536 dimensions, that is about 15 billion multiply-adds per query. Vector systems use ANN indexes instead, and extensions like pgvector add them to PostgreSQL.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Storage and execution engines are built for pages of scalar tuples, not SIMD-friendly float arrays, quantized codes or graph traversal.
  • SQL has no native notion of ORDER BY similarity LIMIT 10 with a recall guarantee.
  • ANN indexes (HNSW graphs, IVF cluster probing, PQ compression) examine a tiny fraction of vectors per query, giving millisecond search at 95 to 99 percent recall.
  • Nuance: pgvector with HNSW or IVFFlat is production-viable for single-digit millions of vectors.
  • At very large scale, dedicated vector databases still win on performance, memory management and operational features.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Where would you stop using pgvector and move to a dedicated store?
  • Why does the curse of dimensionality hurt KD-trees?
💡 Tip: Say 'not natively' rather than 'cannot'; the pgvector nuance earns credibility.

Q9What is FAISS?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you know it is a library, not a database?
  • Can you name its index families and tuning knobs?
  • Can you say when to prototype on it and when to move on?
🗣️ Say this

FAISS, Facebook AI Similarity Search, is an open-source C++ library with Python bindings from Meta for similarity search and clustering of dense vectors. It offers exact flat indexes, IVF clustering, HNSW graphs and product quantization, plus combinations like IVF+PQ for billion-scale search, with optional GPU acceleration. It is not a database: no metadata filtering, no real CRUD, no replication, no auth, no server. So I prototype on FAISS and move to a vector database or pgvector when I need filters, multi-tenancy and operations.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Indexes: IndexFlatL2/IP (exact), IVF, HNSW, PQ/OPQ (compress vectors 10 to 100x).
  • Knobs: nlist and nprobe for IVF, efConstruction and efSearch for HNSW, to trade recall, speed and memory.
  • Utilities include k-means clustering and PCA.
  • Persistence is manual save and load, and deletions are awkward.
  • Best fit: research, offline batch jobs, single-node services, or as the engine inside a larger system. LangChain and LlamaIndex ship FAISS integrations.

Q10What is Pinecone?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you describe it as managed, serverless vector DB and list features like namespaces and filtering?
  • Do you state trade-offs honestly (cloud-only, cost, less control)?
  • Can you position it against FAISS, pgvector and open-source options?
🗣️ Say this

Pinecone is a fully managed, cloud-native vector database offered as an API. You create an index, upsert vectors with metadata and query; it handles sharding, replication and scaling. Its serverless design separates storage from compute so you pay for what you store and use. Namespaces give per-tenant isolation, metadata filters run alongside the ANN search, and it supports hybrid dense plus sparse search. The trade-offs are that it is proprietary and cloud-only, can get expensive at scale, and gives less control over index internals than FAISS or self-hosted engines.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Operational maturity: SLAs, encryption, SOC 2, live updates without downtime, LangChain and LlamaIndex integrations.
  • Cloud-only is a blocker for strict data residency or air-gapped environments.
  • Positioning: Pinecone = managed convenience; FAISS = embedded library with full control; pgvector = keep it in PostgreSQL while small; Weaviate, Qdrant and Milvus = open-source middle ground with self-host or cloud options.

Weaviate is an open-source, cloud-native vector database written in Go, available self-hosted or as a managed cloud. Q12What is chunking in RAG systems?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you explain why we chunk (model limits, one vector cannot hold many topics, context budget)?
  • Can you list several strategies and when each fits?
  • Do you see chunking as a quality driver, not plumbing?
🗣️ Say this

Chunking splits source documents into smaller pieces before embedding and indexing, because you cannot embed a 100-page PDF as one vector and expect useful retrieval. Embedding models have input limits, one vector cannot represent fifty topics, and the LLM context window is finite. The chunk is the unit of retrieval. A good baseline is fixed-size chunks of 500 to 1,000 tokens with 10 to 20 percent overlap, or recursive splitting on paragraphs, then sentences, then words. Better options respect structure, split semantically, or index small chunks but return their larger parent.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Fixed-size with overlap: simple, fast baseline; overlap keeps boundary-straddling sentences in at least one chunk.
  • Recursive character splitting (LangChain's default): paragraph, then sentence, then word, within a size limit.
  • Structure-aware: split by Markdown headings, HTML sections, PDF layout or code functions; store the heading path as metadata.
  • Semantic chunking: cut where embedding similarity between consecutive sentences drops; better coherence, higher cost.
  • Parent-child (small-to-big): search small chunks, return the larger parent section.
  • Contextual enrichment: prepend an LLM-generated summary to each chunk before embedding so 'the rate increased to 8 percent' stays findable.
  • Store metadata (source, title, section, page, tenant) for filtering and citations.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How would you chunk a table-heavy PDF?
  • What is parent-child retrieval?
💡 Tip: Say that chunking quality is the most underrated determinant of RAG quality.

Q13Why is chunk size important?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you frame it as precision of matching versus completeness of context?
  • Do you cover embedding dilution, context budget, cost and answer quality?
  • Do you say it is a hyperparameter to evaluate, not a magic number?
🗣️ Say this

Chunk size controls the trade-off between how precisely the retriever can match and how much context the LLM receives. Small chunks give sharp vectors and precise hits but may lack surrounding facts. Large chunks carry full context but their vectors average many topics, so ranking gets fuzzy and irrelevant text rides along, which also costs tokens on every request. A fixed context budget also decides how many distinct sources fit. A starting point is 300 to 800 tokens with 10 to 20 percent overlap, then measure hit rate and answer quality on your own data.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Embedding fidelity: a small chunk's vector is sharp; a huge chunk's vector is a blurry average (semantic dilution).
  • Budget: five 400-token chunks from five documents often beat one 2,000-token chunk for multi-faceted questions.
  • Quality: filler in big chunks distracts the model (lost in the middle); missing context in tiny chunks makes it guess.
  • Cost and latency: bigger chunks mean more input tokens and slower responses on every query.
  • Starting points: function or class level for code, section level for structured documents.
  • Parent-child retrieval sidesteps the dilemma by searching small and reading big.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How would you evaluate chunk sizes experimentally?
  • How does overlap help and what does it cost?
💡 Tip: Name the experiment: sweep sizes, measure retrieval hit rate and answer quality.
Q14What challenges arise from very large chunks?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you split the answer into retrieval-side and generation-side problems?
  • Do you know silent truncation by the embedding model?
  • Do you offer mitigations?
🗣️ Say this

Very large chunks hurt retrieval and generation. On retrieval, one vector must summarise thousands of tokens on many topics, so it becomes a blurry average that scores mediocre against specific queries, and scores compress so top-k gets noisy. Embedding models also have input limits, commonly 512 to 8,192 tokens, so oversized chunks are silently truncated and the tail is never represented. On generation, a few giant chunks eat the context budget, the key sentence gets lost in the middle, the model is distracted, and cost and latency rise. Mitigate by capping size, splitting on structure, using parent-child retrieval and reranking.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Lost in the middle: LLMs attend best to the start and end of long contexts.
  • Context exhaustion limits retrieval diversity, hurting multi-document questions most.
  • Citation imprecision: a 5-page chunk is barely a citation, so auditability drops.
  • Further mitigations: add a reranker to recover precision; compress retrieved chunks (extractive summarisation) before injection.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How would you detect silent truncation?
  • How does a reranker help with oversized chunks?
📚 References
  • Lost in the Middle (Liu et al.)
Q15What challenges arise from very small chunks?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you describe context fragmentation with a concrete orphaned-sentence example?
  • Do you cover system costs: index bloat, higher k, redundancy?
  • Do you list mitigations (overlap, sentence window, parent-child, structure)?
🗣️ Say this

Tiny chunks lose context. A one-sentence chunk like 'This rate increased to 12 percent in the second year' matches a query but is useless, because the product and the rate live in a neighbouring chunk. Tables get split from headers, code mid-function, pronouns lose their antecedents, so the LLM gets true but insufficient evidence and may guess. System-wise you get ten times more vectors, slower indexing, a need for higher k, and near-duplicate fragments crowding the top results. Fixes: sensible minimum sizes, overlap, sentence-window or parent-child retrieval, and structure-aware splitting.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Rarely go below about 200 tokens for prose.
  • Sentence-window retrieval: retrieve a sentence, return it with surrounding sentences.
  • Parent-child: search small, return the parent section.
  • Structure-aware splitters refuse to break tables, code blocks or list items.
  • Heavy overlap between tiny chunks creates duplicates; deduplicate with maximal marginal relevance (MMR).
  • Higher k re-spends the tokens you hoped to save and raises the chance of irrelevant hits.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • What is maximal marginal relevance?
  • How does sentence-window retrieval differ from parent-child?
💡 Tip: Symmetry wins: large chunks fail at retrieval, small chunks fail at generation; the cure is to decouple the retrieval unit from the context unit (small-to-big).

Q1What is RAG?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you define it precisely and name both halves: retrieval system plus generator?
  • Do you know the offline indexing flow versus the online query flow?
  • Red flag: describing RAG as 'training the model on our documents'.
🗣️ Say this

Retrieval-Augmented Generation combines a search system with an LLM. At question time, the system first retrieves the most relevant pieces of text from an external knowledge base, puts them into the prompt as context, and the model writes an answer grounded in that evidence. The idea comes from Lewis et al. in 2020. The knowledge stays outside the model, so it can be updated in minutes, cited, and filtered by who is allowed to see it. For example, a support bot embeds the customer's question, fetches the top refund-policy chunks, and answers from them with citations.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Core insight: parametric knowledge (in the weights) is frozen, generic and unverifiable, but in-context reading and reasoning is excellent. RAG keeps facts outside and lets the model synthesize.
  • Offline: load documents -> chunk -> embed -> store vectors plus metadata in a vector database.
  • Online: embed question -> similarity search for top-k -> optionally rerank/filter -> assemble prompt (instructions + chunks + question) -> generate with citations.
  • Typical uses: enterprise knowledge assistants, support bots, legal and policy Q&A, documentation copilots.
  • Usually the first thing to try before fine-tuning: cheaper, updatable by re-indexing one document, auditable through citations, and permission-aware.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How is RAG different from fine-tuning?
  • Walk me through what happens between the user pressing enter and the answer appearing.
💡 Tip: Say 'offline indexing, online retrieval and generation' early; it shows you see two separate pipelines.
📚 References
  • Lewis et al. 2020 (the RAG paper)
Q2Why do we need RAG when we already have LLMs?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you name concrete structural limits of a bare LLM, not just 'it hallucinates'?
  • Can you argue against the alternatives (fine-tuning, giant context windows)?
  • Red flag: claiming a long context window makes RAG obsolete.
🗣️ Say this

A standalone LLM has four structural gaps. It has a knowledge cutoff, so it knows nothing recent. It has never seen your private data, like contracts or tickets. When it lacks knowledge it hallucinates a plausible answer. And its answers cannot be verified. RAG fixes all four: retrieval reads from a live index, covers private data under access control, grounds the answer in real text, and returns citations. In one line, LLMs are frozen, generic and unverifiable; RAG makes them current, private and citable.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Fine-tuning is a poor database: costly, slow to update (every document change means retraining), unreliable for factual recall, with no citations or per-user access control. It shapes behavior, not knowledge.
  • Stuffing everything into context: a corporate knowledge base does not fit even in million-token windows, attention degrades ('lost in the middle'), and you pay for every token on every call.
  • Long context and RAG are complements: retrieve first, then use a generous window for what you retrieved.
  • Cost of RAG: you must build and operate a retrieval pipeline and keep it fresh.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • When would you choose fine-tuning over RAG?
  • If the context window were unlimited and free, would you still need RAG?
💡 Tip: Quote the one-line summary; it is memorable and complete.
📚 References
  • Liu et al. 2023: Lost in the Middle
Q3What are the main components of a RAG pipeline?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you organize components into ingestion, retrieval, generation and evaluation?
  • Do you remember the unglamorous parts: parsing, metadata, sync jobs, guardrails?
  • Signal: mentioning evaluation and observability unprompted.
🗣️ Say this

I group it into four layers. Ingestion, offline: loaders and parsers, cleaning, a chunker with metadata, an embedding model, and a vector store, often with a keyword index too, plus sync jobs to keep it fresh. Retrieval, online: query rewriting, the retriever with filters, a reranker, and context assembly. Generation: a prompt template with grounding rules, the LLM, and guardrails like citation checks. Cutting across all of them is evaluation and tracing, measuring recall, faithfulness, latency and cost.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Ingestion: PDF/HTML/Confluence loaders, OCR, table extraction; dedupe and PII handling; chunks tagged with source, section, tenant, permissions, timestamps.
  • Embedding choices: OpenAI text-embedding-3, Cohere, BGE/E5. Stores: Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, pgvector, optionally a BM25 index for hybrid.
  • Retrieval: query rewriting from chat history, decomposition, HyDE, metadata filters; cross-encoder reranker (Cohere Rerank, BGE-reranker); dedupe, order and trim to token budget.
  • Generation: system rules (grounding, citation format, refusal policy) + context + question; post-checks for groundedness and PII.
  • Evaluation: hit rate, recall@k, MRR; faithfulness and answer relevance (Ragas); tracing with LangSmith or Langfuse; user feedback loops.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Which component would you build first, and which would you measure first?
  • Where does freshness break in this pipeline?
💡 Tip: Draw four boxes while you talk; interviewers like a structure they can follow.
Q4What is document retrieval?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you see retrieval as the biggest determinant of answer quality?
  • Can you list the steps: query understanding, embedding, search, filtering, rerank?
  • Signal: knowing to measure retrieval separately from generation.
🗣️ Say this

Document retrieval is the runtime step that, given a query, finds the most relevant chunks in the indexed knowledge base. It is the R in RAG, and it caps answer quality: if the right chunk is not retrieved, no prompt can rescue the answer. The query is optionally rewritten, embedded with the same model used at indexing, searched by nearest neighbors alongside keyword search, filtered by metadata and permissions, then reranked and trimmed. When a RAG system fails, my first question is always: was the right chunk even retrieved?

🔍 Go deeper
  • Query understanding: resolve pronouns from chat history ('what about its pricing?' -> 'what is the product's pricing?'), expand abbreviations, split multi-part questions.
  • Use the same embedding model for query and documents, otherwise vectors are not comparable.
  • Hybrid setups run BM25 in parallel and fuse with Reciprocal Rank Fusion.
  • Metadata filters (tenant, permissions, type, recency) are applied inside the search, not after, or top-k may be emptied by post-filtering.
  • Levers: embedding model, chunking, k, hybrid weighting, reranker, query rewriting. Metrics: recall@k / hit rate, precision@k, MRR, nDCG.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How would you measure retrieval quality without any LLM involved?
  • Why apply filters inside the search instead of afterwards?
💡 Tip: Mention that you evaluate retrieval on its own with a labeled set of question -> gold chunk pairs.
Q5What is document indexing?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you separate offline indexing from online retrieval?
  • Do you know the operational pain: incremental sync, deletions, re-embedding?
  • Red flag: forgetting that changing the embedding model forces a full re-index.
🗣️ Say this

Indexing is the offline process of turning raw documents into searchable structures, mainly embedding vectors in an approximate nearest neighbor index, plus metadata and often a keyword index, so that queries take milliseconds instead of scanning everything. The steps are load and parse, clean, chunk, enrich with metadata, embed in batches, and upsert into the stores. It is the library catalog; retrieval is looking up a book. The big operational risk is stale data, so you sync incrementally and delete vectors of removed documents.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Parsing: PDFs, DOCX, HTML, spreadsheets, transcripts; OCR scans; preserve headings and tables.
  • Metadata per chunk: source URI, title, section path, author, timestamps, language, tenant_id, access tags. Optional per-chunk summaries or context (contextual retrieval).
  • Writes: vector DB builds an HNSW or IVF index; update BM25 index for hybrid; keep raw chunk text in a document store for prompts and citations.
  • Stale indexes are the most common silent RAG failure: watch sources, re-embed only what changed, delete removed docs.
  • Embedding-model upgrades need full re-indexing because vectors from different models are incompatible; use blue-green index swaps.
  • Scale: embedding cost, rate limits, and index build parameters that trade build time against recall.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How would you handle a document that was updated or deleted?
  • How do you roll out a new embedding model with zero downtime?
💡 Tip: Contrast crisply: indexing optimizes for searchability before anyone asks; retrieval consumes it per query.

Q6What is hybrid search?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you explain why keyword and vector search fail in opposite ways?
  • Do you know how to fuse results (RRF, weighted blending, rerank on union)?
  • Signal: giving an example query where pure vectors would miss.
🗣️ Say this

Hybrid search runs keyword search, usually BM25, and vector search in parallel on the same query, then fuses the two ranked lists. Keywords nail exact terms but miss paraphrases; vectors capture meaning but blur exact identifiers like error codes or SKUs. The most common fusion is Reciprocal Rank Fusion, where each document scores the sum of 1 over rank plus c, with c around 60, across the lists. For a query like 'fix error 0x80070057 when updating', BM25 anchors the code and vectors capture the intent.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • RRF is rank-based, so no score normalization is needed: score(d) = sum over lists of 1/(rank + c), c about 60. Documents ranked well in both lists rise to the top.
  • Weighted blending: alpha * vector_score + (1 - alpha) * BM25_score after normalizing; alpha tunes lexical versus semantic per corpus (Weaviate exposes this).
  • Rerank-on-union: take candidates from both retrievers and let a cross-encoder decide the final order; increasingly the production default.
  • Wins on identifiers inside natural language, jargon, product names, legal citations, people's names, and out-of-domain text.
  • Support: Weaviate, Qdrant, Pinecone, Elasticsearch/OpenSearch, Azure AI Search natively; with pgvector combine Postgres full-text search and vector search, fusing with RRF in SQL or code.
  • Cost: two indexes and two searches, almost always worth it.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Why does RRF not need score normalization?
  • How would you choose alpha for a new corpus?
💡 Tip: Work one tiny RRF example on a whiteboard: rank 1 and rank 3 beats rank 2 and nowhere.
📚 References
  • Anthropic: Contextual Retrieval (hybrid + rerank results)
Q7What is reranking in RAG systems?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you understand bi-encoder versus cross-encoder and the speed/accuracy trade?
  • Do you know typical numbers (retrieve 25-100, keep 3-8)?
  • Signal: the recall-versus-precision framing.
🗣️ Say this

Reranking is a second-stage scorer. The first stage, vector or hybrid search, quickly fetches a generous candidate set, say the top 50. A slower, smarter model then rescores each candidate against the query, and only the best three to eight go into the prompt. The reranker is typically a cross-encoder that reads the query and the chunk together, so it catches negation and exact conditions that cosine similarity smears over. In short: retrieval optimizes recall, do not miss the answer; reranking optimizes precision, do not drown it.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Bi-encoder: query and document are embedded independently, then compared. Fast and scalable but the query never reads the document, so fine distinctions (annual-plan refunds versus monthly-plan refunds) are lost.
  • Cross-encoder: query plus candidate go through one Transformer so attention compares them token by token. Too slow for millions of documents, fine for 50.
  • Options: hosted (Cohere Rerank, Voyage, Jina), open source (BGE-reranker, mxbai-rerank, MiniLM cross-encoders), or an LLM scoring candidates (flexible, pricier).
  • Typical pattern: hybrid top-50 -> rerank -> top-5 -> generate, adding roughly 50-300 ms and often the biggest single precision gain.
  • Side effects: fewer chunks mean fewer prompt tokens, less distraction-driven hallucination, and the best chunk can be placed first against lost-in-the-middle.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Why not use the cross-encoder for the whole corpus?
  • How would you decide how many candidates to rerank?
💡 Tip: Close with the recall/precision one-liner; it is the line interviewers remember.
Q8How does RAG help reduce hallucinations?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you explain the mechanism (task change from recall to reading), not just 'it adds facts'?
  • Do you volunteer the caveats: RAG reduces but does not eliminate?
  • Red flag: saying RAG eliminates hallucination.
🗣️ Say this

Hallucination happens because next-token prediction always produces something plausible, even beyond what the model reliably knows. RAG changes the task from recalling facts from the weights to reading these passages and answering from them, which models do far more reliably. Relevant text in context makes the truthful continuation the most probable one. Add an instruction like 'answer only from the context, otherwise say you don't know', and require citations so claims are checkable. But it reduces, not eliminates: if retrieval returns the wrong chunks, the model confidently grounds on bad evidence.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Grounding: authoritative evidence in context dominates the model's prior guesses.
  • Refusal instruction turns confident fabrication into a visible 'I don't know'.
  • Citations let users and validators check each claim against its chunk, so residual errors are caught.
  • Many 'hallucinations' are stale or generic knowledge; retrieval supplies current, company-specific truth.
  • Verification layer: groundedness / faithfulness scoring with an LLM judge or NLI model checking each answer sentence is entailed by context (Ragas faithfulness) can gate answers.
  • Caveats: misreading and over-synthesis, garbage-in-garbage-out retrieval, conflicting sources (use recency filters, authority weighting, dedup).
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How would you measure whether your RAG answers are faithful?
  • What do you do when the retrieved chunks contradict each other?
💡 Tip: State the caveats yourself before they ask; it signals production experience.
Q9What is context injection in RAG?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Do you know that how you inject context changes quality, not just what you retrieve?
  • Delimiting, ordering, budgeting, dedup: can you name them?
  • Signal: connecting delimiting to prompt-injection defense.
🗣️ Say this

Context injection is the step where retrieved chunks are physically inserted into the LLM prompt, the moment retrieval results become model input. A good template has a system instruction, such as answer strictly from the documents, cite as numbered sources, say you don't know otherwise, then the chunks wrapped in tags with an id and source, then the question. I order the strongest chunks first, enforce a token budget, and drop near-duplicates. Nothing in the model changes; you are programming it per request with evidence.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Delimitation: tag each chunk with ID and source for citations, stop chunks bleeding together, and separate data from instructions (retrieved text is content, never commands; a prompt-injection defense).
  • Ordering: strongest chunks at the start and/or end to fight lost-in-the-middle; reranker scores drive order.
  • Budgeting: cap context tokens, trim lowest-ranked chunks, optionally compress to extractive snippets.
  • Dedup and diversity (MMR) so the budget buys distinct evidence.
  • Chat: rewrite the query with history before retrieval so injected context reflects the full intent.
  • Prompt caching: keep static instructions in a stable prefix, inject variable context after it, to cut cost and latency.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How do you protect against a malicious document that contains instructions?
  • How many chunks would you inject and why?
💡 Tip: Draw the template with doc tags; concrete beats abstract.

Q10What is the difference between chunking and windowing?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Can you distinguish what is stored and matched from what is shown to the model?
  • Do you know sentence-window and parent-child retrieval?
  • Trap: confusing it with sliding-window attention only.
🗣️ Say this

Chunking partitions a document once, at indexing time, into independent pieces, say 500 tokens with 10 to 20 percent overlap. Each chunk is embedded, retrieved and cited as a unit. Windowing, in RAG, means retrieving a small unit but returning it with its surrounding neighbors. In sentence-window retrieval you index single sentences for precise matching, then give the model each hit plus a few sentences before and after. Chunking decides what is matched; windowing decides what the model sees. It lets you match small and read big.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Chunking: fixed boundaries, so a fact straddling a boundary relies on overlap; one size must serve both precision (small is better) and completeness (big is better).
  • Sliding window (general): a fixed view moves with stride smaller than its size, giving overlapping views; used for long-text embedding, classification, and sliding-window attention in models like Mistral.
  • Sentence-window retrieval (e.g., LlamaIndex): retrieval unit is a sentence, context unit is the window of N neighbors.
  • Parent-child retrieval generalizes it: search small child chunks, hand the LLM the larger parent section.
  • Cost: extra bookkeeping to store neighbors or parents and expand at query time, usually repaid in both precision and answer completeness.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How would you pick chunk size and overlap?
  • What are the downsides of returning a larger parent section?
💡 Tip: Use the phrase 'match small, read big'.

Q1What is an AI agent?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • A crisp definition: LLM plus tools plus a loop plus autonomy over control flow
  • The agent versus workflow distinction (who decides the steps)
  • Mentions guardrails: step limits, budgets, permissions, human approval
🗣️ Say this

An AI agent is a system where an LLM is given a goal and a set of tools, and decides at runtime what to do next, in a loop, until the goal is met. The loop is: perceive the goal and context, reason about the next step, act by emitting a tool call, observe the result, and repeat. The model is the brain, tools are the hands, memory carries state. For example, a support agent looks up an order, issues a refund through an API, and emails the customer. In production I add step limits, budget caps, scoped tool permissions and human approval for irreversible actions.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Autonomy: the LLM chooses steps at runtime. In a workflow the developer hard-codes the path; Anthropic draws exactly this line in Building Effective Agents
  • ReAct style interleaves a thought, an action and an observation, so each step is grounded in real results
  • Defining properties: autonomy, tool use with real side effects, state across steps, and success measured by task completion not fluent text
  • Examples: coding agent that edits files and reruns tests until green; research agent that searches and synthesizes; support agent that acts through APIs
  • The loop ends when the model decides it is done or a step or cost limit is hit
  • Serious answers include guardrails: step caps, budget caps, least-privilege tools, approval gates

Q2How is an AI agent different from a chatbot?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Control flow, side effects and success criterion as the axes of difference
  • Awareness that agents fail by doing the wrong thing, not just saying it
  • Nuance: it is a spectrum, not a binary
🗣️ Say this

A chatbot converses; an agent accomplishes. A chatbot takes one message and returns text. An agent runs a multi-step loop, calling tools with real side effects, and the LLM decides the steps and when to stop. A chatbot keeps a transcript; an agent keeps task state like plans and intermediate results. Success for a chatbot is a helpful reply; for an agent it is a completed task, such as a refund issued. Ask a chatbot about an order and it tells you where to look; an agent calls the orders API, sees the problem, opens a ticket and reports back.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Interaction: single shot per turn versus dozens of reason, act, observe cycles per task
  • Output: text only versus executed actions such as writing files, calling APIs, completing transactions
  • Control flow: developer-coded path versus model-chosen path at runtime
  • State: transcript only versus plans, scratchpads, and often long-term memory
  • Failure mode: a chatbot says something wrong; an agent does something wrong, so permissions, approval gates and observability matter far more
  • In between: a chatbot with RAG and one or two tools is a partial agent; full agency is when the model owns the loop

Q3What is tool calling?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • The model never executes anything; it emits a structured request
  • Can walk the round-trip step by step
  • Knows where security lives (validation in your code) and has heard of MCP
🗣️ Say this

Tool calling lets an LLM request external functions. You describe each tool with a name, purpose and parameter schema. When the model decides it needs one, it emits a structured call, usually JSON with the tool name and arguments. Importantly, the model executes nothing. Your application validates and runs the function, then sends the result back as a tool-result message, and the model continues or answers. For example the model emits get_weather with city Bengaluru, my code calls the weather API, and the model phrases the answer. That separation is also where security lives.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Round-trip: send messages plus tool schemas, model returns text or tool_use calls, your code executes, you append results and call again, repeat until a final answer
  • Loops are allowed and are exactly how agents work
  • Parallel tool calls let the model request several independent calls in one turn
  • Supported natively by OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini and open models such as Llama, Qwen and Mistral through trained tool-call formats
  • Model Context Protocol (MCP) standardizes how tool servers expose tools to many hosts, often described as USB-C for AI tools
  • Tool calling doubles as structured output: define a schema as a tool and the model fills it
↪ Likely follow-ups

  • What is MCP and why does it matter?

Q4Why do agents need external tools?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Names the specific LLM limits: stale knowledge, no private data, weak exact arithmetic, no actions
  • Judgment versus capability framing
  • Security corollary: tools expand blast radius
🗣️ Say this

A bare LLM is a frozen text predictor. It cannot know anything after its training cutoff, cannot see your systems, cannot reliably do exact arithmetic, and cannot change the world. Tools fix each gap: search and retrieval give fresh facts, calculators and code interpreters give exact answers, APIs let it send emails or create tickets, and test runners let it verify its own work. The model supplies judgment, the tools supply capability. Because every tool widens the blast radius, I use least-privilege credentials, read-only defaults and approval for irreversible actions.

👀 See it

  • Ground truth: an LLM multiplying 8-digit numbers is pattern matching; a calculator tool is arithmetic
  • Action: send email, update a CRM, drive a browser, turning answers into outcomes
  • Perception of private state: calendar, codebase, order database, logs
  • Verification is the biggest reliability unlock: run the code it wrote, re-query after a write, detect and fix its own errors
  • Tool design is a first-class skill: clear names, tight schemas, good descriptions and helpful error messages raise success rates

Q5What is function calling in LLMs?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know

Function calling is the API feature behind tool calling. You pass the model function definitions, each with a name, a natural-language description and a JSON Schema for parameters. Q6What is agent memory?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Starts from the fact that LLMs are stateless
  • Can structure memory types: working, episodic, semantic, procedural
  • Names engineering challenges: what to write, when to retrieve, how to forget
🗣️ Say this

Agent memory is how an agent retains and recalls information within a task, across a long conversation, and across sessions. It exists because LLMs are stateless: each call only knows the tokens you send. So memory is engineered around the model, deciding what to store, where, and what to load back into the context. I describe four kinds: working memory is the current context window; episodic memory is records of past interactions; semantic memory is distilled facts like 'customer is on the Enterprise plan'; procedural memory is reusable playbooks and skills.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Vector-store memory: embed past notes and retrieve relevant ones per query, which is RAG over the agent's own history
  • Summarization or compaction keeps old conversation inside the context budget
  • Structured stores: key-value profiles, SQL or graph databases, and files the agent reads and edits
  • Productized layers: LangGraph checkpointers and stores, Mem0, Zep, MemGPT/Letta
  • Core challenges: salience (what to write), relevance (when to retrieve), staleness and contradiction (how to update or forget), and privacy
↪ Likely follow-ups

  • How do you handle contradicting memories?

Q7What is short-term memory in agents?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Equates it with the context window; if it is not in context the model cannot see it
  • Knows the cost and capacity trade-off
  • Lists management techniques and failure modes
🗣️ Say this

Short-term or working memory is everything in the active context window for the current task: the system prompt, recent turns, the agent's scratchpad reasoning, the plan, and tool results so far. It is the only memory the model literally sees. Within the window recall is perfect, but capacity is limited and you pay for every token resent at each step. It disappears when the task ends unless saved. To manage it I use sliding windows, summarizing old content into a compact note, pinning the goal and key decisions, and dropping bulky raw tool outputs.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Capacity ranges from about 8K to 1M tokens depending on the model, and cost grows because the whole history is resent every step
  • Each loop iteration appends its observation, so short-term memory is the glue that makes multi-step reasoning coherent
  • Truncation is simple but forgets abruptly; compaction summarizes older content and replaces it, as Claude Code does
  • Selective retention: keep the conclusion of a 10K-token search result rather than the raw dump
  • Externalize then retrieve: write details to files and fetch them only when needed (context engineering)
  • Prompt caching on a stable prefix avoids re-paying for unchanged tokens
  • Failure modes: context overflow, context rot from a bloated middle, and goal drift after aggressive truncation
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • What is context rot?
  • How would you decide what to pin versus compress?
💡 Tip: Name at least two failure modes; most candidates only name overflow.
Q8What is long-term memory in agents?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Persistence outside the context window with selective loading
  • Can describe both the write path and the read path
  • Calls out maintenance: decay, privacy, contradiction handling
🗣️ Say this

Long-term memory is information stored outside the context window, in databases, vector stores, files or graphs, that survives across sessions and is loaded back only when relevant. It holds user facts and preferences, summaries of past sessions, learned procedures and documents. On the write path, the agent or a background job extracts salient facts, deduplicates, resolves contradictions where the newer fact wins, and stores them with embeddings and metadata. On the read path, relevant memories are retrieved by similarity plus recency and importance, then injected into the prompt.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Stores: user facts (semantic), interaction history (episodic), task artifacts and playbooks, and shared organizational knowledge such as the RAG corpus
  • Retrieval scoring combining similarity, recency and importance was popularized by the Generative Agents paper
  • Maintenance: decay or forgetting for stale facts, user-visible privacy controls, audits
  • Unbounded accumulation degrades retrieval quality and creates compliance risk
  • Implementations: vector DB collections, Mem0, Zep, LangGraph stores, MemGPT/Letta paged memory, or simple profile tables plus RAG
  • Contrast: short-term is in-context, complete, ephemeral and token-priced; long-term is external, selective, persistent and retrieval-priced. The craft is the interface between them
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How do you stop stale memories from misleading the agent?
  • What privacy controls would you add?
💡 Tip: Present write path, read path, maintenance as three beats.

Q10What are the advantages of multi-agent systems?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Quality advantages and operational advantages separated
  • Balanced view including costs
  • A decision heuristic for when to go multi-agent
🗣️ Say this

The main advantages are specialization, context separation, parallelism and built-in checks. Each agent has a focused prompt, a small tool set, and maybe a model suited to its job, so it follows instructions better than one giant prompt. Each works in its own context window holding only what it needs. Independent subtasks like researching five competitors run in parallel, cutting wall-clock time. Generator and critic pairs catch errors a single agent misses. The costs are real: multiplied token spend, latency and harder debugging. So I start with one agent and split only when the task decomposes.

👀 See it

  • Separate contexts sidestep overflow and distraction that cripple monolithic agents on large tasks
  • Debate and proposer-verifier setups measurably improve reasoning accuracy
  • Operational wins: modularity like microservices, cost routing, fault isolation with per-agent traces, permission scoping so dangerous tools sit behind one guarded agent
  • Costs: multiplied tokens, coordination latency, error propagation, harder debugging
  • Go multi-agent when subtasks are parallelizable or distinct, one context cannot hold the work, or a critic demonstrably improves quality

Q1What is fine-tuning?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • A precise definition: continue training a pre-trained model on a small, specific dataset so its weights adapt.
  • Knowing the scope options: full fine-tuning, parameter-efficient (LoRA), preference tuning.
  • The key trap: fine-tuning is for behavior, not for injecting fresh, citable facts.
🗣️ Say this

Fine-tuning means continuing the training of an already pre-trained model on a smaller, task-specific dataset, so its weights shift toward what you need. The model already knows language and the world; fine-tuning teaches it your output format, tone or a narrow task using thousands of examples instead of trillions of tokens. In practice you train on prompt and ideal-answer pairs, with a low learning rate for one to three epochs. For example, I would fine-tune a small model to always emit a strict JSON schema for support tickets. For facts that change, I would not fine-tune; I would use RAG.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Data is usually supervised (prompt, ideal completion) pairs formatted as chat transcripts; loss is next-token cross-entropy computed only on the target response tokens.
  • Low learning rate and 1-3 epochs avoid catastrophic forgetting, where new training overwrites pre-trained knowledge.
  • Scope: full fine-tuning updates every weight (strongest, costliest, most forgetting); PEFT such as LoRA or adapters updates a tiny fraction and is today's default; RLHF/DPO optimize against preferences rather than exact targets.
  • Good at: style and tone, strict formats, classification, extraction, SQL generation, domain vocabulary, shrinking prompts, and distilling a big model into a small cheap one.
  • Poor at: knowledge that must be current and citable. Facts baked into weights are hard to update and cannot be cited. Production pattern: fine-tune for behavior, RAG for knowledge.
  • Tooling: hosted fine-tuning APIs, or open-weight models (Llama, Mistral, Qwen) with Hugging Face PEFT/TRL, Axolotl, Unsloth, LLaMA-Factory.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • What is catastrophic forgetting and how do you limit it?
  • Why compute the loss only on the assistant tokens?
  • Would you fine-tune to teach the model your company's product catalog?
💡 Tip: Say the one-liner: fine-tune for behavior, RAG for knowledge.

Q2When should you choose fine-tuning over prompt engineering?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • The escalation ladder: prompting, then few-shot, then RAG (if the gap is knowledge), then fine-tuning.
  • Evidence-driven decisions: evals justify each step, not enthusiasm.
  • Awareness of economics at scale (prompt tokens paid on every call) and of when NOT to fine-tune.
🗣️ Say this

My default is an escalation ladder: prompt engineering, then few-shot examples, then RAG if the gap is knowledge, and only then fine-tuning. I fine-tune when cheaper rungs demonstrably fail on my eval set, when behavior must be rigidly consistent, or when scale makes it cheaper, for example deleting a 2,000-token instruction block that is paid for on every call, or replacing a frontier model with a tuned small one. I stay with prompting when requirements change weekly, when data is scarce, or when I have no evals. Prompting says what to do, RAG says what to know, fine-tuning changes what the model is.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Choose fine-tuning when prompting has plateaued on a measured eval set after serious iteration.
  • Deep consistency: brand voice, tutor persona, rigid output schema across millions of requests.
  • Cost and latency at scale: moving instructions and few-shot examples into weights removes tokens paid per call; distillation to a small model often decides the case.
  • Unteachable by instruction: specialized notation (medical coding, legal citation), subtle judgments learned from many examples.
  • Small or open models must reach quality for on-prem, edge or privacy needs; a tuned 8B model often beats a prompted 8B on a narrow task.
  • Stay with prompting or RAG when requirements change fast, the gap is knowledge, data is scarce or noisy, or you lack eval discipline.

  • How many examples do you need before fine-tuning is worth trying?

Q3What is LoRA?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Core mechanism: freeze W, learn a low-rank update delta W = B times A.
  • Parameter math you can do on a whiteboard (d x k versus r x (d + k)).
  • Practical properties: merge for zero inference overhead, swappable adapters.
🗣️ Say this

LoRA, Low-Rank Adaptation by Hu et al., is a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method. It freezes all original weights and, next to selected weight matrices, adds two small trainable matrices. Instead of learning a full update delta-W of size d by k, it learns B of size d by r and A of size r by k, with a tiny rank r like 8. The layer computes W x plus alpha over r times B A x. For a 4096 by 4096 matrix, full delta-W is 16.7 million parameters; rank 8 trains only about 65 thousand, roughly 0.4 percent.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Intrinsic-rank hypothesis: the weight change needed to adapt a pre-trained model lies in a low-dimensional subspace, so rank 8 captures most of what full fine-tuning would learn for a format or style.
  • Parameter math: r x (d + k) = 8 x 8192 = 65,536 versus d x k = 16.7M. Over a 7B model, trainable parameters fall to roughly 10-40M (under 1%).
  • No optimizer state for frozen weights, so memory drops roughly 3x versus full fine-tuning; a 7B model trains on one consumer GPU.
  • Zero inference overhead: merge W' = W + B A after training, so the deployed model is as fast as the original.
  • Swappable adapters of tens of MB: one base model in memory, adapters hot-swapped per customer or task (vLLM, LoRAX).
  • Hyperparameters: rank r, scaling alpha (commonly about 2r), target modules (q/k/v/o and often MLP layers), dropout. B is initialized to zero so training starts from the base model.
  • Quality usually matches full fine-tuning; large behavior shifts or heavy new-knowledge injection can still favor full fine-tuning. Same trick is used for Stable Diffusion style adapters.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Why is B initialized to zero and A random?
  • How do you choose rank and alpha?
  • Which layers do you attach LoRA to, and why?
💡 Tip: Do the 4096 x 4096 arithmetic out loud; it shows you understand it rather than recite it.

Q4What is QLoRA?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Definition: 4-bit quantized frozen base plus LoRA adapters trained in higher precision.
  • The three innovations: NF4, double quantization, paged optimizers.
  • The trade-off: less memory but slower steps.
🗣️ Say this

QLoRA, by Dettmers et al., combines a 4-bit quantized frozen base model with LoRA adapters trained in higher precision. The base weights are stored in a 4-bit format called NF4 and dequantized to bf16 on the fly for each computation; gradients flow through them into the adapters, which are the only trained parameters. The paper fine-tuned a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU with quality matching 16-bit fine-tuning. For a 7B model, memory falls from roughly 100 GB for full fine-tuning to about 5-6 GB for the base, so a free Colab T4 can do it.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • NF4 (4-bit NormalFloat): quantization levels placed at quantiles of a normal distribution, which matches the shape of pre-trained weights and keeps more signal than uniform int4.
  • Double quantization: the quantization constants are themselves quantized, saving about 0.4 bits per parameter, which matters at 65B.
  • Paged optimizers: optimizer states page between GPU and CPU memory like OS virtual memory, avoiding out-of-memory crashes on spikes.
  • Cost: roughly 30-50% slower per step than plain LoRA because of dequantization. You trade time for memory.
  • Quality: minimal degradation in the paper's tested scales.
  • Deployment: merge the adapter into a dequantized base for full-precision serving, or keep the 4-bit base plus adapter for cheap inference.
  • Tooling: bitsandbytes with Hugging Face PEFT and transformers (load_in_4bit), Unsloth, Axolotl.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Why does QLoRA train slower than LoRA?
  • Can you merge a QLoRA adapter into the 4-bit weights directly?
  • When would you prefer plain LoRA over QLoRA?
💡 Tip: If you have the memory, plain LoRA is faster; choose QLoRA when memory is the binding constraint.
📚 References
  • QLoRA paper (Dettmers et al., 2023)
Q5Why is LoRA more efficient than full fine-tuning?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Memory accounting: weights + gradients + optimizer states, about 16 bytes per parameter for full fine-tuning.
  • Benefits beyond memory: storage, multi-tenant serving, less forgetting.
  • A balanced caveat about where full fine-tuning still wins.
🗣️ Say this

Full fine-tuning pays for every parameter several times over; LoRA pays for almost none. For N parameters, full fine-tuning holds the weights, a gradient for each, and Adam's two moments, roughly 16 bytes per parameter, about 112 GB for a 7B model before activations. LoRA freezes the base, so gradients and optimizer states exist only for the roughly 0.1 to 1 percent adapter parameters, and a 7B run fits on one 24 GB GPU. It also produces tens-of-MB adapters instead of 14 GB model copies, and forgets less. The caveat is that very large domain shifts can still favor full fine-tuning.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Memory per parameter in full fine-tuning: 2 bytes bf16 weights + 2 bytes gradients + about 8 bytes Adam states in fp32 (plus often an fp32 master copy), about 16 bytes total.
  • LoRA keeps only the frozen weights at full cost; QLoRA shrinks those a further 4x.
  • Compute: the backward pass updates millions rather than billions of parameters; small datasets converge fast, so jobs take hours rather than days.
  • Storage: ten tasks become ten small adapters and one shared base instead of ten 14 GB copies.
  • Serving: one base model in GPU memory serves many adapters, hot-swapped or batched per request; merged adapters add zero latency.
  • Frozen weights mean less catastrophic forgetting, and the small parameter count acts as regularization on small datasets.
  • Caveat: for a very large and very different corpus (new language, deep new knowledge), the low-rank constraint caps expressiveness and full fine-tuning can win.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • Walk me through the memory for a 7B model, term by term.
  • How does multi-adapter serving work in production?
  • When would you still do full fine-tuning?
💡 Tip: Name the three memory terms (weights, gradients, optimizer states); most candidates only mention weights.
Q6What factors should be considered while preparing a fine-tuning dataset?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Quality over quantity (LIMA: about 1,000 excellent examples can beat many mediocre ones).
  • Representativeness of production traffic, edge cases, consistency, deduplication.
  • Hygiene: train/validation/test splits without leakage, licensing, PII.
🗣️ Say this

Dataset quality is the biggest driver of fine-tuning success, much more than hyperparameters, because the model learns every flaw in the data. I check four things. Correctness and consistency: every target is exactly what I want, with uniform format and tone, deduplicated, no PII and no contradictory pairs. Coverage: it matches real production traffic, includes edge cases and out-of-scope requests with correct refusals, and is balanced across classes. Format: it follows the model's chat template, with loss on assistant turns. Hygiene: test splits are made before training with no leakage, and licensing is clear.

🔍 Go deeper
  • Evidence: the LIMA paper showed about 1,000 carefully curated examples can produce strong alignment; quality beats quantity at every scale.
  • Consistency: inconsistent labels, JSON schemas or tone teach randomness. Remove near-duplicates and same-input/different-output contradictions.
  • Representativeness: use real user phrasings, document types and lengths; training only on clean lab inputs fails on messy reality.
  • Edge cases and hard negatives: ambiguous inputs, adversarial phrasing, out-of-scope requests with the right refusal.
  • Size guidance: style or format adaptation about 100-1,000 examples; task specialization commonly 1K-50K.
  • Leakage: carve out validation and test sets first and ensure near-duplicates do not cross splits, or metrics will be fake.
  • Synthetic data from a stronger LLM plus human review is standard for bootstrapping; check provider terms and watch for narrowing of the distribution.
  • Treat it as a loop: train, evaluate, mine failures, add targeted examples, retrain.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How do you detect near-duplicate leakage between train and test?
  • How would you build a dataset when you have no labeled data?
  • What happens if 30 percent of your examples contain a formatting inconsistency?
💡 Tip: Say: the model becomes what it eats, and it will reproduce your worst examples, not your best.
📚 References
  • LIMA paper (Zhou et al., 2023)
Q7How do you evaluate a fine-tuned model?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Two questions: did the target task improve, and did anything else break?
  • Baselines: the fine-tune must beat the best-prompted base model.
  • A layered protocol: held-out set, metrics, LLM judge validated by humans, regression and safety checks, online rollout.
🗣️ Say this

I answer two questions: did the target task improve, and did anything else break. First, a held-out test set from the production distribution, verified leak-free. Second, baselines: the fine-tune must beat the base model with my best prompt, zero-shot and few-shot, otherwise the GPUs were wasted. Third, task metrics such as exact match, F1, schema validity or pass rate, plus an LLM judge with a rubric validated against human ratings, and expert review on a sample. Then regression and safety checks for forgetting, and finally shadow testing and gradual A/B rollout with latency, cost and online metrics.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Task metrics: exact match, accuracy, F1 for classification and extraction; schema-validity rate; pass@k and unit-test pass rate for code; execution accuracy for SQL; BLEU or ROUGE only as weak signals for free text.
  • LLM-as-a-judge: grade against a rubric or compare pairwise against the baseline; randomize position to avoid bias and validate the judge on a human-rated sample.
  • Human evaluation by domain experts on a stratified sample is the gold standard for tone, safety and domain correctness.
  • Regression checks: run general benchmarks and out-of-domain prompts to catch catastrophic forgetting; fine-tuning can erode refusal behavior even unintentionally.
  • Robustness: paraphrases, adversarial inputs, long inputs, out-of-scope requests.
  • Operational: latency, throughput and cost versus the alternative; shadow testing then A/B ramp; continuous monitoring with failures feeding the next training set.
  • During training: validation loss rising while training loss falls signals overfitting; stop early or reduce epochs.
↪ Likely follow-ups
  • How do you validate that your LLM judge is trustworthy?
  • How would you test whether safety behavior regressed?
  • What would make you decide not to ship a fine-tune that scored better?
💡 Tip: Always mention the prompted-baseline comparison and a regression/safety check; those separate seniors from juniors.
📚 References
  • Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023)
Q8What metrics can be used to measure model performance?
🎯 What the interviewer wants to know
  • Metrics matched to the task type, not a single universal score.
  • Awareness of weak metrics (BLEU/ROUGE) versus stronger ones (judge, human preference).
  • Operational metrics (latency, cost) alongside quality.
🗣️ Say this

It depends on the task, so I group metrics. In training, I watch cross-entropy loss on train and validation and perplexity, which is the exponent of loss. For classification and extraction I use accuracy, precision, recall and F1, with confusion matrices under class imbalance, and schema validity. For generation, BLEU and ROUGE are cheap but weak, BERTScore is better, and LLM-judge rubric scores or pairwise win rates validated by humans are the practical standard. For code, pass@k; for RAG, faithfulness, context precision and recall. Finally, latency, cost and online success rate decide production viability.

👀 See it
🔍 Go deeper
  • Training: cross-entropy loss for diagnosing under- and overfitting; perplexity = exp(loss) compares language-model fit but is a weak proxy for task usefulness.
  • Classification and extraction: accuracy, per-class and macro/micro precision, recall and F1; confusion matrix; schema validity and field-level exact match.
  • Generation: BLEU (n-gram precision) and ROUGE (n-gram or LCS recall) correlate loosely with quality; METEOR and BERTScore capture semantics better; LLM-judge scores and human preference are the best signals.
  • Code and agents: pass@k via unit tests (HumanEval style), execution accuracy for SQL, task completion rate, tool-call correctness, steps to completion.
  • RAG (Ragas): faithfulness, answer relevance, context precision and recall; retrieval hit rate, recall@k, MRR, nDCG.
  • Safety: refusal correctness, jailbreak resistance, toxicity and bias scores.
  • Operational: time-to-first-token, tokens per second, cost per 1K requests, error rate; online task success, deflection, CSAT, escalation rate.
↪ Likely follow-ups

  • What is pass@k and how is it estimated?
  • Which single metric would you report to an executive, and why?
💡 Tip: Closing pattern: one north-star task metric, an LLM-judge rubric validated by humans, regression and safety guards, and latency and cost tracking.
